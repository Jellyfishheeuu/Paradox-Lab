Dark Matter

The invisible substance that makes up 85% of the universe's mass. What is it, and why can't we see it?

📹 CASE TRAILER
🎧 AUDIO NARRATION

Dark matter is one of the greatest mysteries in modern physics. It doesn't emit, absorb, or reflect light, making it invisible to telescopes. Yet its gravitational effects are undeniable. Without dark matter, galaxies would fly apart, and the universe as we know it wouldn't exist.

The evidence for dark matter began accumulating in the 1930s when astronomer Fritz Zwicky noticed that the Coma Cluster of galaxies was moving too fast to be held together by visible matter alone. Since then, observations of galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, and the cosmic microwave background have all pointed to the existence of this mysterious substance.

What We Know About Dark Matter:

1. It's Abundant: Dark matter makes up about 27% of the universe's total mass-energy content, compared to 5% for ordinary matter and 68% for dark energy.

2. It's Invisible: It doesn't interact with electromagnetic radiation, which is why we can't see it directly.

3. It's Cold: Dark matter particles move slowly compared to the speed of light, making it "cold" dark matter.

4. It's Everywhere: It's distributed throughout the universe, forming halos around galaxies and clusters.

Candidate Particles:

1. WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles): Hypothetical particles that interact via gravity and the weak nuclear force. They could be detected in underground experiments like LUX-ZEPLIN or XENON.

2. Axions: Lightweight particles that could solve the strong CP problem in quantum chromodynamics. Experiments like ADMX are searching for them.

3. Sterile Neutrinos: Hypothetical neutrinos that don't interact via the weak force. They could explain some astrophysical observations.

Alternative Theories:

1. Modified Gravity: Theories like MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics) suggest that gravity behaves differently at large scales, eliminating the need for dark matter.

2. Emergent Gravity: Some quantum gravity theories propose that gravity emerges from other fundamental forces, potentially explaining galactic dynamics without dark matter.

The Detection Challenge:

Detecting dark matter is extremely difficult because it interacts so weakly with ordinary matter. Current experiments are pushing the boundaries of sensitivity, but so far, no direct detection has been confirmed. Indirect detection through gamma rays or cosmic rays from dark matter annihilation is also being pursued.

The existence of dark matter challenges our understanding of the universe. If it's made of unknown particles, it would require new physics beyond the Standard Model. If it's something else entirely, it could revolutionize our conception of gravity and the cosmos.
