The Trolley Problem

A moral dilemma that forces us to choose between equally unacceptable outcomes.

📹 CASE TRAILER
🎧 AUDIO NARRATION

The trolley problem is a thought experiment in ethics that illustrates the tension between utilitarianism and deontological ethics. It was first proposed by philosopher Philippa Foot in 1967 and has since become a cornerstone of moral philosophy.

The Classic Scenario:

A runaway trolley is heading down a track toward five people who are tied to the rails and unable to move. You are standing next to a lever that can switch the trolley to a side track. However, there is one person tied to the side track. Should you pull the lever, sacrificing one life to save five?

Variations:

The Fat Man: Instead of a lever, you could push a large man off a bridge onto the tracks to stop the trolley. This introduces the distinction between action and inaction.

The Loop: The side track loops back to the main track, potentially killing the one person and then continuing to kill the five.

The Surgeon: A surgeon could kill one healthy patient to harvest organs that would save five dying patients.

Ethical Theories:

Utilitarianism: The morally right action is the one that maximizes overall happiness or minimizes suffering. Pulling the lever saves more lives.

Deontology: Certain actions are inherently wrong, regardless of consequences. Actively causing harm (pulling the lever or pushing the man) is murder.

Virtue Ethics: Focuses on the character of the person making the decision rather than the action itself.

Real-World Applications:

The trolley problem has implications for:

• Autonomous Vehicles: How should self-driving cars be programmed to handle unavoidable accidents?

• Medical Ethics: Resource allocation during pandemics or organ transplants.

• Military Decisions: Bombing campaigns that minimize civilian casualties.

• Climate Change: Policies that benefit some at the expense of others.

The trolley problem reveals the complexity of moral decision-making. It shows that there are no easy answers to difficult choices, and that our intuitions about right and wrong can conflict with each other.

Your investigation confronts the uncomfortable reality of moral trade-offs. What would you do in the trolley driver's seat? How do we decide between competing moral imperatives?
