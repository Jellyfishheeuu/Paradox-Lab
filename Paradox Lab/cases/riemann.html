The Riemann Hypothesis

One of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics—the distribution of prime numbers.

📹 CASE TRAILER
🎧 AUDIO NARRATION

The Riemann Hypothesis, proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, is one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics. It concerns the distribution of prime numbers and the zeros of the Riemann zeta function.

Prime numbers are the building blocks of arithmetic—numbers greater than 1 that have no positive divisors other than 1 and themselves. The distribution of primes has fascinated mathematicians for centuries, and Riemann's hypothesis provides a key insight into their behavior.

The Zeta Function:

The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) is defined for complex numbers s as the sum of 1/n^s for all positive integers n. Riemann discovered that the function has "trivial" zeros at negative even integers, and possibly "non-trivial" zeros elsewhere.

The Hypothesis:

Riemann hypothesized that all non-trivial zeros of the zeta function lie on the "critical line" where the real part of s is 1/2. This seemingly simple statement has profound implications for number theory and has resisted proof for over 150 years.

Why It Matters:

If the Riemann Hypothesis is true, it would provide deep insights into the distribution of prime numbers. It would also have implications for cryptography, quantum physics, and our understanding of the fundamental nature of numbers.

Attempts at Proof:

Mathematicians have made significant progress, proving the hypothesis for a large percentage of zeros, but a complete proof remains elusive. The problem is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, with a $1 million reward for its solution.

The Riemann Hypothesis represents the frontier of mathematical knowledge. Solving it would not only answer a centuries-old question but also open new avenues of mathematical exploration. It reminds us that even in the realm of pure abstraction, mysteries persist.
